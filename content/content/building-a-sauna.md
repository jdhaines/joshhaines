## Introduction

For many years I've wanted to have a traditional Finnish-style sauna.  I got close
back in 2022 and did all the usual sauna dreamer research:

- Bought the e-book from [SaunaTimes](https://www.saunatimes.com/build-your-own-sauna-ebook/?srsltid=AfmBOoo39kFbAzBadcNnwnsz5BxHdgY-wVZR970qwGue56Z-EZvmJI17)
- Read every post on the [Sauna Reddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/Sauna/)
- Watched every YouTube video I could find on building a sauna

Finally, in March 2026 I decided to take the plunge and kick off the build. At a
high level:

- I had a 8' x 16' shed delivered for an 8x8 changing room and an 8x8 sauna room.
  It has an extra 2' of height to allow tall benches and 9' interior ceilings.
- I insulated the building, build a divider wall, and installed electrical wiring.
- I tiled the floor with extra friction porcelain tile with no drain.
- I added pine car siding to the ceiling in the changing room and reclaimed barn
planks on the walls.
- I added the door, foil vapor barrier, and cedar to the sauna room.
- I added the Iki Classic Plus wood burning stove and chimney through the roof.
- I built the benches from clear cedar and added backrests.

Follow along below as we go through each section of the process including costs,
lessons learned, changes I might make if building again, and photos throughout the
process.

## Utility Shed

I went to a local shed dealer in town and ordered a standard 8' x 16' utility shed
with no windows, a single door on the end, painted red with white trim.  I had them
add an extra 2' of height to the shed as I need
ed the extra room for taller benches because I chose the Iki Classic Plus stove
which is relatively tall.  With the extra 2 feet, the interior ceiling height was
9' from the floor to the rafters.

The shed itself was about $4800 after taxes with $350 being the up-charge for the
extra height.

## Initial Interior Work

The first thing I did on the shed is install 3/4" OSB on the floor.  The floor
already had a painted/sealed OSB flooring, but I needed it thicker to support the
tiles I was going to install later.  I used a lot of screws to ensure it was secure.

Next I build the divider wall between the changing room and sauna room.  I used
2x4s for the studs and got the rough door opening from the retailer.  I needed to
have the door as close to the stove as possible to maximize the room for the
benches later so the door is a little off-center.  The studs and OSB were
about $100 or so.

## Insulation

In alignment with the general consensus with the sauna community, I chose fiberglass batt insulation for the walls and ceiling
in the changing room and mineral wool for the sauna room. I used 6" batts in the ceiling and normal 4" batts for the walls.
Mostly this work was done as you would expect.  The fiberglass batts had paper and I used staples.  The mineral wool was very
heavy, but was friction fit and just stayed in place in the walls.

On the ceiling things got interesting.  I ordered [steel rods](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D9BP51SG) to hold the insulation in the ceiling.  This worked okay for the fiberglass, although a couple times the whole row would fall and make a mess. I wouldn't use these again.  They failed completely for the mineral wool.  The rods were too thin and the mineral wool was too heavy.

I ended up seeing a random post where someone said to use staples and twine.  I tried this and it worked great.  I used a
staple gun to staple the twine to the ceiling joists in a zig-zag pattern, then stuffing the insulation above it.  This worked perfectly and I would recommend this method for anyone else doing mineral wool in a ceiling.

In the second picture you can see the metal rods holding the fiberglass (barely).  In the second and third picture below you can see the
twine holding the mineral wool perfectly (at the top).  All-in, the insulation was around $650.

## Electrical

I took a lot of time considering how to do the electrical.  The shed is located about 125' from my pool which has a separate
circuit and GFCI protection.  I decided, for now, to avoid trenching a new permanent line.  Instead I used a [shore inlet](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08CQH2N7D) fixture so that I could plug in the building with an extension cord.  I didn't want power to be required to use the sauna, but it is certainly nice for some things.

The 100' and 50' extension cords I bought were rated for 15 amp cold weather use so all the other electrical fixtures (outlets, switches) were rated for 15 amps as well.  The circuit is already outdoor protected so I could skip that.  I ran the power in from the inlet to a junction box with an access panel in case I ever wanted to re-wire to something more permanent.  I then added:

- 2 outlets in the changing room (phone chargers, maybe a speaker)
- 3 switches
    - overhead changing room light
    - outside porch lights
    - outside bright spot-light to light the path from the house to the sauna
- 1 switch at the sauna door to control the sauna light
- 1 outlet in the sauna room under the bench in case I want to install LED sauna lights for underglow (this is also switched
with the sauna light switch)

With this setup I think I'll have all the flexibility I'll need.  Also, the sauna is out near our fire pit so the changing
room can be a nice staging place where we could put a table, crock pots, or other things to support bonfire parties.  In those
cases the power will be handy.

In the winter when I'm not mowing, I can just leave the building plugged in.  If we ever don't want to run power, we can
simply bring a lantern with us and that plus the wood burning fire light should be plenty to use the sauna.

In the future I may choose to run permanent power or install solar power or something, but for now I've been happy with it.
Having the power available for work lights and power tools during construction has been fantastic.

delivered.  I ended up working with [Bsaunas USA](https://bsaunasusa.com/) for my kit.  I talked with Demitri and Alex at different times and they were super helpful. ## Tiling the Floor

This was the hardest (and scariest) part of the build for me.  I had done a bunch on construction with my Dad when I was
younger so most of this wasn't too big of a deal... I was rusty but I had seen it before.  I had never done tile work before
though and I was nervous about it.  I watched a lot of YouTube videos and read a lot of articles to try and prepare.

### Underlayment and Preparation

I had decided not to put in a drain.  I plan to pour water on the stones, but not bathe or dump buckets in the sauna.  I
wanted some basic water protection in case we ever still the water bucket and for things to stay protected until I could grab
a mop.  I also wanted the floor to be easy to clean and not too slippery.

I put cement board 6" high on the walls in the sauna room so that I could make it almost a waterproof bathtub. To give the tile the best chance of not cracking or coming loose, I decided to use a decoupling membrane.  I chose [Schluter Ditra](https://www.schluter.com/schluter-us/en_US/Membranes/Uncoupling-(DITRA)/Schluter%C2%AE-DITRA-&-DITRA-XL/p/DITRA) which is a 1/8" thick membrane that is installed with mortar and then the tile is installed on top of it.  Specifically, this is suitable to go right over OSB or plywood so I didn't need to put down cement board.  This product allows for some movement in the floor without cracking the tile. I also chose [Schluter All-Set](https://www.schluter.com/schluter-us/en_US/Setting-Materials/Schluter-ALL-SET%C2%AE/p/SET_ALL-SET) which is a modified thinset mortar that is designed to be used with the Ditra membrane.  I then used [Schluter Kerdi-Band](https://www.schluter.com/schluter-us/en_US/Membranes/Waterproofing-(KERDI)/Schluter%C2%AE-KERDI-BAND/p/KERDI_BAND) to seal the corners and between pieces on the Ditra seams.  I used a Schluter trowel to spread the mortar and a rubber float to press the membrane into the mortar.

This stuff wasn't cheap.  A big roll of Ditra was about $320, two bags of All-Set was about $80, Kerdi corners were $60, and the Kerdi band was another $80 so the pre-tile flooring was about about $550 by itself.  If it prevents the tiles from cracking after years of use it will all be worth it though.

### Tile Installation

For the tile I ended up going with [Mohawk StepSure in 12x24](https://www.menards.com/main/flooring-rugs/tile-stone/porcelain-tile/mohawk-reg-stepsure-trade-urban-forge-trade-divine-12-x-24-matte-porcelain-floor-and-wall-tile/uf051224xtmtmn/p-1642874365961701-c-6557.htm) in the light Divine color.  This tile has additional grip and friction when wet which seemed great for a sauna. I got the grout color they had on the display which was [Power Grout](https://www.menards.com/main/flooring-rugs/tile-stone/tile-installation-maintenance/tile-grout-caulk/tec-reg-power-grout-reg-550-7-lbs/7077398011/p-1444432160151-c-6582.htm) in Silverado.  I used a standard tile scorer/breaker to cut the tiles.  It was a learning experience and I mis-cut a few but mostly things went okay.

I used 3/8" tile spacers and a standard wedge brand of tile leveling system.
I had a couple small mistakes, but nothing too major.  I laid out the tiles with the spacers starting at the front door.  This
meant I had a partial tile in the back under the benches.  It came out perfectly with the final row of tiles exactly at the
front of the building with a small gap.  I used a few mis-cut
tiles for the wall under the benches, but you'd have to look hard to notice.
Most of my mistakes were simply time cost.

In the first half the job I left
too much mortar between the tiles which meant I had to grind it out with a
grout saw.  Later I figured that out so there wasn't much clean-up.  I also
had a few tiles that were slightly off-level, but nothing too bad.  Overall
I'm happy with the way it turned out and think it looks really nice with the
darker wood on the walls and ceiling.

9 boxes of tile were about $450 and 3 bags of grout were $60 bringing the
total tile cost to about $510.  I also bought a tile cutter for about $100
and a few other small tools and supplies for about $50, although I'm not
including tools in the total.  I had some tools, but needed some new ones
that were specific.

## Door and Changing Room

### Sauna Door

The door was buried near the bottom of the box of cedar and I was a
little worried they forgot to include it.  It was nicely protected and
in great shape when I opened it though.  I had to cut the rough opening
a little larger as forgot to consider the height of the underlayment,
mortar, and tile in the rough opening (oops!).  I put it in, shimmed it,
and screwed it in place.  It opens and closes really smoothly and even
has a nice magnetic latch to keep it closed.
